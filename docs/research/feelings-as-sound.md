# Feelings as sound — the research, from every field it touches

For `tools/music/planned/feeling-song.spec` ADDENDUM 2026-11-13 (2). His words (2026-11-13): "look at all research into this, from all
domains." · "i want love, anger which is low irratating pitch, sadness, like hanging tree or bite the hand that feeds you."

Read from abstracts and summaries (searched 2026-11-13), not every full text; where a finding is contested it says so. What ERAVOS takes
from each is in **bold**. Nothing here is a claim about a body: the tool measures its sound, never a listener.

## 1. Emotion psychology — how to lay feelings out

- **Valence × arousal beats named emotions for music.** Eerola & Vuoskoski (2011, *Psychology of Music* 39(1)) compared models on 110 film
  excerpts: the two-dimensional model held up, and named emotions handled ambiguous excerpts poorly. **The pad is continuous: the four corners
  are named, everything between them is a blend — the in-between is where the ambiguous, real feelings are.**
- **Speech and music share one code.** Juslin & Laukka (2003, *Psychological Bulletin* 129(5)), 104 vocal + 41 music studies: the same cues
  (pitch level, loudness, tempo / rate, timbre, articulation) carry the same emotions in both. **Each corner is defined by those cues, so
  the corners sound like voices feeling something, not like presets.**

## 2. Animal communication — why low and harsh means anger

- **Morton (1977, *American Naturalist* 111): motivation-structural rules.** Across birds and mammals, hostile close-range calls are LOW and
  HARSH (broadband); friendly or appeasing ones are HIGH and TONAL. The root is body size: low = big = dangerous. **This is his "anger which
  is low irritating pitch" exactly: Anger is low and harsh; Love is higher and pure.**
- **Filippi et al. (2017, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 284): arousal heard across species.** People hear higher arousal in calls of amphibians,
  reptiles, birds and mammals, in three languages — by fundamental frequency and spectral centre of gravity. **The drag's speed and the
  dot's height raise pitch and brightness: arousal is universal.**

## 3. Psychoacoustics and neuroscience — what "irritating" is

- **Roughness, 30–150 Hz.** Arnal et al. (2015, *Current Biology*): screams sit in a privileged niche — loudness modulated 30–150 Hz
  ("roughness"); alarms and dissonant intervals share it; the rougher, the more disturbing; it drives the amygdala. **Anger's irritation is
  made of roughness: partials placed so they beat inside 30–150 Hz, measured in the sound.**
- **Dissonance is beating between partials.** Helmholtz → Plomp & Levelt → Sethares (*Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale*): sum the beating of
  every pair of partials; the minima of that curve are the consonant intervals FOR THAT SOUND. An inharmonic (irrational) timbre has its own
  consonances, off the 12 notes (gamelan is predicted this way). **The key to irrational frequencies that are beautiful, not just strange:
  Love tunes to the minima of its own timbre's dissonance curve; Anger to its peaks.**
- **Chills (frisson).** Sloboda (1991), Panksepp (1995), Grewe et al. (2007): sudden changes, crescendos, rising loudness and sharpness;
  Huron: violated expectation. **A fast drag across corners is a sudden change — the moments of most feeling are the crossings.**
- **Sub-audible sound.** Angliss, Wiseman, Lord et al. (2003, the Soundless Music concerts): 17 Hz under music raised strange feelings
  (sorrow, chills, unease) by ~22 % in a double-blind test — one study, small. **Not used: nothing below 25 Hz. A low "felt" layer
  25–40 Hz is offered for Sadness and Anger, under its own control, off by default.**
- **Binaural beats: not used.** Ingendoh et al. (2023, *PLOS ONE*) systematic review: 5 of 14 studies support entrainment, 8 contradict.

## 4. Speech and voice — what sad and tender sound like

- **Sad = lower, smaller, darker, mumbled, bent.** Huron (2008): sad (minor) themes lower, smaller intervals; Huron, Anderson & Shanahan
  (2014): instruments that sound sad play quieter, slower, lower, darker, mumble-like — and in a re-analysis, **pitch-bending** was the one
  cue that predicted sadness capacity. **Sadness glides and sags between pitches; it never steps cleanly.**
- **Minor thirds in sad speech: contested.** Curtis & Bharucha (2010) found them; Buxó-Lugo & Slevc (2021) did not. Bowling et al. (2010):
  major thirds far more common in excited than subdued speech. **No interval is hard-wired to a feeling; intervals come from the timbre's
  own consonances (§3).**
- **Voice quality.** Gobl & Ní Chasaide (2003, *Speech Communication*); Yanushevskaya et al. (2005): breathy voice pairs with sadness,
  lax-creaky with intimate / relaxed / low activation, pressed-breathy tracks arousal. **Love is soft-onset and slightly breathy; Sadness
  breathy and dark; Anger pressed and buzzing.**

## 5. Song across cultures — love is the hardest to hear

- Mehr et al. (2019, *Science*), 60 societies: lullabies, dance, healing and love songs exist everywhere and are told apart by accent,
  tempo and pitch range — but love songs are the hardest to identify ("acoustically fuzzy"). **Love is not one cue: it is tenderness
  (slow, soft, legato, warm, consonant) plus closeness (near, centred). His ear sets it.**

## 6. Synthesis and games — a pad with four corners

- **Vector synthesis.** Sequential Prophet VS (1986): four oscillators at the corners of a diamond, a joystick blends them live; carried to
  Yamaha SY22 / TG33 and Korg Wavestation. **His "four way grid": the tradition exists and works. ERAVOS goes further — not four waveforms
  mixed, but four feelings whose every cue (pitch, roughness, tuning, onset, bend, space) moves between them.**
- **Adaptive game music.** Vertical layering and crossfades driven by an arousal–valence state (arXiv 2207.01698; SFU thesis): stems share
  tempo and key so any blend stays coherent. **The four corners share one clock and one root, so every point between them is playable.**

## 7. The polyvagal frame — use the words, make no claim

- Porges's polyvagal theory (ventral / sympathetic / dorsal) is widely used in practice; its physiology is contested — Grossman (2023,
  *Biological Psychology* 178) argues its core premises are untenable; Neuhuber & Berthoud (2022) likewise; Porges's institute disputes them.
  **The tool says nothing about anyone's nervous system. For embodiment it offers what the research supports: sound that carries a feeling,
  played by your own hand, and always a way back (Return).**
